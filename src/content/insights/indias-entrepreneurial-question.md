India does not lack people who can imagine starting a business. The more difficult question is what helps a promising idea survive contact with customers, operating constraints and time.

The latest India profile from the [Global Entrepreneurship Monitor (GEM)](https://gemconsortium.org/economy-profiles/india), drawing on its 2025 data, offers a useful starting point. More than one in four adults expect to start a business within three years. At the same time, around one in eight adults are already starting or running a business. GEM also reports that more than half of people who see good local opportunities would not start a business because they fear it may fail.

These figures describe different measures, not a conversion funnel. They should not be divided into one another or read as a forecast of how many intended businesses will launch. They do, however, point to a question worth taking seriously: what sits between recognising an opportunity and building an enterprise that can keep serving customers?

## The gap is not simply ambition

Entrepreneurial intention is a signal of interest. Early-stage activity is a signal that someone has begun. Neither, on its own, tells us whether a business has found repeat demand, can deliver consistently, generates enough cash to sustain itself or has a credible route to growth.

GEM's profile makes a second distinction important. It reports that established business ownership in India is about half the rate of early-stage entrepreneurial activity. The measures have specific definitions: GEM counts established businesses as those that have paid salaries, wages or other payments to their owners for more than 42 months. The comparison is therefore about stages of business activity, not a simple survival rate for a single cohort.

The practical implication is not that new businesses are destined to fail. It is that the conditions required to start a business are not identical to the conditions required to sustain one.

A founder may be able to identify a need and assemble an initial offer, yet still face unanswered questions about repeat purchasing, working capital, quality, distribution, hiring, compliance or the economics of serving a wider market. Those questions differ by sector and geography. They cannot be resolved by enthusiasm or a broad market-size estimate.

## Opportunity recognition needs a test

The GEM profile reports that more than half of adults who see good opportunities locally would not start a business because of fear of failure. That finding should not be dismissed as a confidence problem. In many cases, caution may be rational: starting can involve personal financial exposure, uncertain demand and limited room to absorb a mistake.

The response should not be to encourage every person with an idea to take a bigger risk. It should be to make the next decision more informed.

A useful early test asks:

- **Who experiences the problem?** Describe a specific customer or user, not an entire population.
- **What do they do today?** Existing workarounds reveal what customers value, tolerate and already pay for.
- **What would make them change?** A solution needs a reason to displace current behaviour.
- **What evidence would change the decision?** Decide in advance what would strengthen, weaken or invalidate the idea.
- **Can the offer be delivered repeatedly?** A first sale is encouraging; reliable delivery and repeat demand are different tests.

These questions do not remove uncertainty. They make it visible, so a founder can choose a proportionate experiment rather than commit too much too early.

## Growth requires more than a larger market

The GEM profile also highlights two areas that deserve closer attention. It reports that only around one in twenty new entrepreneurs in India have customers outside the country, despite an increase in that proportion in 2025. It also reports that only around one in twenty expect to employ more than five additional people over the following five years.

These are not measures of business quality, and international customers or rapid hiring are not appropriate goals for every enterprise. A focused local business can be successful without either. The figures do, however, invite more precise questions about what growth means for a particular business.

For one enterprise, the next step may be to serve a neighbouring district more reliably. For another, it may be to meet the standards required by a larger institutional buyer. A third may have a product that can travel across borders, but needs stronger distribution, certification, service or fulfilment before it is ready.

The relevant question is not simply, "How large is the market?" It is, "What capabilities must this business develop to serve the customers it actually intends to reach?"

That moves the conversation from market aspiration to operating reality.

## A more disciplined sequence for building

For founders and the people supporting them, the data reinforces the value of separating several decisions that are often compressed into one:

1. **Understand the problem.** Identify who is affected, how frequently the problem occurs and what it costs them.
2. **Examine the current alternatives.** Map competitors, informal workarounds, substitutes and reasons customers stay with them.
3. **State the business hypothesis.** Explain who would pay, for what outcome, at what price and why the offer could be delivered sustainably.
4. **Run the smallest credible test.** Use customer conversations, prototypes, pilots or paid trials to test the riskiest assumptions.
5. **Review the operating model.** Consider margins, working capital, supply, quality, people, regulation and customer acquisition before scaling.
6. **Decide what should happen next.** Continue, adapt, partner, pause or stop. Each is a legitimate outcome of good analysis.

This is a framework for making decisions, not a guarantee of success. The sequence will vary by sector, product, capital intensity and regulatory context. A manufacturing business, a software product and a local service enterprise will not validate demand in the same way.

## The Tarvanta perspective

At Tarvanta, we believe the quality of a venture depends in part on the quality of the questions asked before it is built. Research should do more than make an idea sound convincing. It should expose assumptions, identify what remains unknown and help determine whether the next step is justified.

India's entrepreneurial energy is important. But the ambition to start is only one part of the story. The deeper work is building businesses that understand their customers, deliver reliably and earn the right to grow.

That is where a promising idea begins to become an enduring venture.
